**Subject:** Build vs buy — LobeChat self-hosted vs SaaS AI assistants

# TL;DR

- Self-hosting wins on cost and flexibility **only if** we genuinely need proprietary RAG, custom MCP tools, and tight control over confidential financial data.
- For a 200-user company, the infrastructure-only 3-year TCO from my Q3 model is about **$38,952** self-hosted versus roughly **$432,000** for ChatGPT Enterprise at the exam assumption of `$60/user/month` and **$216,000** for Microsoft 365 Copilot at Microsoft’s published `$30/user/month`.
- If our real use case is mostly generic productivity inside Word, Excel, Outlook, and Teams, we should buy Copilot instead. The self-hosted route is justified only when workflow control matters more than operational simplicity.

# 3-year TCO comparison (200 users)

Using my Q3 production model, I estimate a self-hosted monthly run rate of about **$1,082** at 200 monthly active users, or **$12,984/year** and **$38,952 over 3 years**. That figure includes AWS infrastructure, storage, observability, and Bedrock inference, but not internal labor. For the SaaS comparators, I use the exam’s assumption of approximately **$60/user/month** for ChatGPT Enterprise and Microsoft’s currently published **$30/user/month** for Microsoft 365 Copilot.

| Option | Monthly cost | 3-year cost | Comment |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Self-hosted LobeChat stack | `$1,082` | `$38,952` | Lowest direct infrastructure/subscription cost, highest operational ownership |
| ChatGPT Enterprise | `200 x $60 = $12,000` | `$432,000` | Premium managed experience, but expensive if many users mostly need internal retrieval and tooling |
| Microsoft 365 Copilot | `200 x $30 = $6,000` | `$216,000` | Much cheaper than ChatGPT Enterprise and operationally simple, but tied strongly to Microsoft’s ecosystem |

This table does **not** mean self-hosting is automatically cheaper in a full economic sense. The missing line is internal operating effort. Even a fraction of a platform/security engineer changes the comparison materially. But the table does prove that if the company needs a custom AI workbench rather than a generic assistant, the cloud run cost itself is not the blocker.

# Lock-in analysis

Self-hosted gives the lowest lock-in across the four dimensions that matter here.

`Data lock-in:` lowest with self-hosted. Documents sit in S3, metadata in Postgres, vectors in Qdrant, and the orchestration layer is ours. Export and migration cost exists, but it is operational rather than contractual. ChatGPT Enterprise is medium lock-in: the workspace is managed, the admin plane is vendor-owned, and some higher-level constructs like workspace knowledge and internal apps are harder to reproduce exactly elsewhere. Copilot is highest because the value of the product is deeply tied to Microsoft Graph, Entra, SharePoint, Teams, and the rest of Microsoft 365.

`Identity lock-in:` self-hosted is lowest because we can keep OIDC/SSO behind Casdoor or another IdP. ChatGPT Enterprise is medium: it supports enterprise identity controls, but the workspace remains separate from our stack. Copilot is highest because identity, permissions, and grounding are tightly coupled to Microsoft’s productivity estate.

`Model lock-in:` self-hosted is lowest because we can route across Bedrock, OpenRouter, or a local provider. ChatGPT Enterprise and Claude Enterprise are much more model-vendor-specific by design. Copilot is also vendor-bound even if it uses OpenAI models under the hood, because the control plane and product experience belong to Microsoft.

`Tooling lock-in:` self-hosted is the clear winner because MCPHub and custom RAG give us our own tool surface. SaaS products increasingly support connectors and agents, but the governance, billing, and extension model remain vendor-shaped.

# Data sovereignty + compliance

For finance, this is where self-hosting has the strongest case. A private-equity workflow can involve confidential deal material, management weaknesses, customer concentration, pricing assumptions, portfolio KPI packs, and internal investment views. Some of this can be commercially sensitive; some may also be regulated or contractually restricted. Running the core retrieval and storage plane in `eu-west-1` gives the firm tighter control over data residency, retention windows, auditability, and segregation between live deals and portfolio monitoring.

That does **not** mean self-hosting is “GDPR-compliant by default.” We would still need a lawful basis, data minimization, retention policy, role-based access, vendor due diligence, and a defensible DPIA where appropriate. But self-hosting improves our control over where data lives, how long it persists, and which model providers can touch it.

SaaS options can also support enterprise compliance, especially Microsoft in a heavily M365-centric company. The trade-off is that the governance boundary moves outward: we accept the vendor’s storage, connector, and admin abstractions rather than defining our own.

# Capability gap

Self-hosted does several things the SaaS products do not do as cleanly:

- native integration with our own MCP tools, not only vendor connectors;
- custom RAG over our own deal corpus and operating playbooks;
- model choice and routing by task;
- ability to keep the retrieval/orchestration stack as a reusable internal platform rather than a chat seat.

This matters if we want a system that behaves like a finance-specific workbench rather than a general assistant.

The SaaS tools still have meaningful advantages:

- stronger out-of-the-box reliability and vendor support;
- better polished end-user UX, mobile access, and admin maturity;
- faster feature refresh;
- much less platform ownership.

Copilot has an additional advantage if most work already lives in Outlook, Teams, PowerPoint, Excel, and SharePoint. ChatGPT Enterprise has strong general-purpose AI breadth. Claude Enterprise has a strong enterprise security story and fast-moving connector surface. None of those strengths disappear just because the self-hosted TCO is lower.

# Recommendation

My recommendation is: **self-host if** three conditions are true.

1. A meaningful share of the value comes from proprietary retrieval and workflow control, not from generic writing assistance.
2. The company can assign clear operational ownership, even if only a quarter-to-half FTE across platform, security, and application stewardship.
3. Data control in a finance context is strategic enough that model flexibility, custom tooling, and retention control justify more complexity.

If those conditions hold, I would choose the self-hosted stack. The direct cost gap versus SaaS is large, and the capability fit is better for private-equity-style knowledge work.

If those conditions do **not** hold, I would buy Microsoft 365 Copilot, not build. For a generic knowledge-worker rollout, Copilot is the most pragmatic “buy” option because it is materially cheaper than the ChatGPT Enterprise assumption in the brief and fits the daily software estate of most companies.

# Reversal trigger

I would reverse this recommendation if, within 6-9 months, one of the following becomes true:

- fewer than `20%` of meaningful user sessions depend on custom RAG, MCP tools, or non-standard workflows;
- the self-hosted platform requires more than `0.5 FTE` of continuous operational attention;
- a SaaS vendor offers comparable private retrieval, enterprise tooling, and policy control at a price still below our fully loaded internal cost.

In other words, I would self-host **for differentiated workflows**, not as a philosophical commitment.
